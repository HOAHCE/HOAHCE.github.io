Students are expected to understand one more thing after every class. The same question should be put to the person at the front of the room: after that class, does the teacher understand more deeply what they have just taught, understand their students better, and see what needs to change?

This question goes to the heart of the teaching profession: teaching is a process of learning at a higher level. When knowledge is passed on to others, the teacher has to verify it, organise it and explain it clearly enough for learners to take it in, question it and put it to use. A concept that seems familiar can reveal its gaps the moment it has to be explained in plain language.

That is why a student's question is worth more than a check on how well the lesson has been understood. It can expose an assumption that was never made clear, an example that did not quite fit, or a perspective the teacher had never considered. Welcoming such questions takes a firm command of one's subject as well as humility before knowledge.

A teacher who is willing to say “this needs to be looked into further” is practising academic honesty. When those words are followed by searching the literature, checking the evidence and coming back with a well-founded answer, students get to see how learning happens in practice. Lifelong learning thus takes on a concrete shape.

Dedication and professional responsibility also show in work that is rarely seen: revising a lecture, reading a piece of student work carefully, updating an example, taking the time to find out why students are falling behind. To sustain all this, lecturers need time and an environment that encourages research, exchange and experimenting with teaching methods. Raising the quality of education is a responsibility shared by teachers and their institutions alike.

A classroom comes alive when knowledge keeps being examined and every question has a chance to be heard. A teacher who is still learning gives students more reason to believe that understanding can always be extended.
